Ladson Stukes, colored-male, of Rt. #3, Box 121-A, Summerton, S. C. was interviewed by the Investigator, J. Leon Gasque, at Nora Felder's house, Rt. #3, Box 18, Summerton, S. C. The investigator explained to him who he was and what he was doing here, with reference to the problems they were having down here in their schools and was here to try to help them get it straightened out. Also about the petition they had signed. The questions and answers are as follows:

Q. Did you sign this petition?

A. When I find out its for better for the children, I find out its for better conditions, I signed it.

Q. When did you sign this paper?

A. This year.

Q. When, back in the summer?

A. I don't remember.

Q. Was it cold or hot?

A. In the summer.

Q. About two months ago?

A. don't remember, like to be plain about it.

Q. Who brought this paper to you to sign?

A. Two men.

Q. Colored men?

A. Yes Sir.

Q. Do you know who they were?

A. I don't remember.

Q. Are you a member of the NAACP?

A. Yes Sir.

Q. Who were these men who brought the paper out, do you know?

A. No, I believe they, he said up the road, I believe he said Chicago. I know they weren't from around here. I know somewhere up the road.

Q. When was the first time that you heard about this last paper?

A. They had a meeting.

Q. Did you go to the meeting?

A. I ain't been to no meeting. I think they had a meeting or something, I ain't gone.

Q. What did they tell you the paper was about?

A. For better conditions for the schools.

Q. Did they tell you it was to send your children to white schools?

A. Wasn't no white schools mentioned, better conditions, signed for better conditions for my children.

Lee Richardson, colored-male, Rt. #1, Box #133, Summerton, was interviewed at
his house on September 7, 1959. The Investigator, J. Leon Gasque, explained
to him that he worked for the State of South and that he understood he had
signed a petition and he just wanted to know about the facts surrounding his
signing the petition. The following questions and answers occurred during the
conversation with Lee Richardson:

Q. Lee when were you approached about signing this petition?
A. I just cant tell, I can't remember.
Q. Who told you about the petition?
A. I can't tell you that, I can't remember, no sir.
Q. You signed the paper didn't you?
A. Yes sir, this is the second one I have signed.
Q. Did you sign a petition?
A. Petition, thats right.
Q. Did Billy Flemming bring it out?
A. No
Q. Who brought the paper out?
A. I can't tell you that, several people came out to my house.
Q. Are you interested in better schools?
A. Thats a question asked me several months ago, after the petition, I am
through with it.
Q. Are you interested in colored children and white children going to school
together?
A. Thats a question I don't know how to answer, go see some of the head men.
Q. Are you interested in your name staying on the petition?
A. Thats what I put it on there for, to stay on there.
Q. Do you want the school to stay like it is?
A. I ain't able to tell you that.
Q. Who do you suggest that I go to see?
A. Some of the officials.
Q. Well then, who are the officials?
A. I don't know, I can't tell you that.
Q. What was your petition for?
A. I can't say.
Q. You can't say or won't say?
A. I don't know.

I went on to tell Lee Richardson that I worked for the State and that I wanted
to find out what his problems were so I could see about them being straightened
out.

Q. Did anybody tell you not to talk to the State men?

A. No, nobody told me not to talk to the State men.

Q. Was it lawyer Jenkins?

William Ragin, colored-male, of Rt. #2, Box #4, Summerton, S. C.,
was interviewed by the Investigator, J. Leon Gasque, at Nora
Felder's house, Rt. #3, Box #18, Summerton, S. C. The investigator
explained to subject who he was and that he understood they were
having some problems down here about the schools. That he had
been sent down here to try to help straighten them out. That he
wasn't here to tell him what his rights were and asked him about
the petition he signed.

Q. William, did you sign a petition?

A. Yes, I did sign a petition.

Q. William, now what are you interested in, this segregation?

A. No, for keeping them together. Integration is what I am for.
Our schools and teachers are no good. Anything I say, I ain't for
segregation.

Q. What seems to be your problem, no good teachers, no equal
rights, your children not getting a good education?

A. You can't have anything equal and separate.

Q. What is wrong with your schools?

A. There is nothing wrong with the schools. We don't have one
word to say about anything and don't know what is going on, and
we are not considered at all.

Q. Just what is your problem, what do you want?

A. To be able to have them, to be able to know who is going to
teach our children.

Q. What is the difference then in where they go to their school or
the white school?

A. I don't know about that.

Q. How do you know it is not worse over there in the white schools?

A. I don't know about that.

Q. Are you interested in the schools being equal or mixing?

A. Mixing and then getting equal rights.

Q. When was this thing brought up to you?

A. I just don't remember, you could go to Columbia and talk to my
lawyer.

Q. Who is your lawyer?

A. Go to Columbia and talk to Jenkins.

Q. Do you know what you signed when you signed the petition?

A. Should.

Q. Did you get a copy of what you signed?

A. Yes.

Q. Did everybody get a copy?

A. I don't know.

William Hilton, colored-male, of Rt. #2, Box 123A, Summerton, S.C.
was interviewed at his home on September 8th, 1959. Investigator,
J. Leon Gasque, explained to him that he worked for the State and
subject asked to see his credentials, of which the Investigator did.
The Investigator explained to him that he was down here to try and
help them get some problems straightened out, that they were having
in their schools. That he had talked to some others and understood
that his name was on the petition and wanted to talk to him. He
told him he was not here to police, but to try to get to the bottom
of their problems. The following are the questions and answers:

Q. What was your understanding as to this petition's meaning?

A. I don't care to comment on it.

Q. Did you sign the petition?

A. I signed of my own free will and accord.

Q. Did you know what this paper contained?

A. I know what it was.

Q. Did you know what it meant?

A. Yes, to integrate.

Q. What is your understanding about integration?

A. Thats where the colored and white go to school together, thats
the way I understood it.

Q. What are your reasons for wanting them to go to school together?

A. Reasons for wanting it for them to go to school together, I
have special reasons, well yes I have a lot of reasons. One is I
believe they will get a better education. I don't believe they
have the teachers, I haven't been in the white schools, I just
don't believe the negroes have the type of teachers they have in
the white schools.

Q. If the courts say the schools are equal, would you still be
interested in sending your children to the white schools?

A. If the courts say they are equal and separate, I think they
could, but thats for the courts.

Q. If the schools were rated as being equal, but separate, would
you be satisfied?

A. I have already signed the petition for integration. I think
I would rather have integration.

Q. Is your main reason for better schools or for mixing the races?

A. That has been, but not since the Supreme Court handed its de-
cision down May 17, 1954.

Q. Is your main interest in education or is your main interest in
mixing the races?

A. Now thats a tough question to answer.

Q. Are you interested in your children getting a better education,
or are you interested in your children going to school with the
white children?

A. I guess the answer to that would be not until we try integration.

Blease C. Gibson, husband of Frances Gibson, petitioner, colored-

In an attempt to locate Frances Gibson, we tried several times at
her home and she wasn't there. At this particular time her hus-
band was there and we talked to him in an effort to find out if
he knew anything about the petition. The investigator, J. Leon
Gasque, explained to him that he had been trying to locate his
wife for three days and told him who he was and the information he
wanted about the petition and the problems in their schools. The
following are the Investigator's questions to Blease Gibson and his
answers:

Q. Blease what is all this trouble you are having here in the
schools? I understand they are trying to put the colored children
in the schools with the white children and its my understanding
that you are not for that.

A. Mister, I don't have time to bother with that, I have enough
trouble.

Q. Your children are your problems too, aren't they?

A. Thats right.

Q. Do you know when this paper was brought around?

A. I see the paper last week for the first time. She say it been
about a year ago, she say.

Q. Where did she sign this paper?

A. I don't know, I asked her when did it happen and she said about
a year ago at some church.

Q. What did she understand the paper was for?

A. I don't know about that. I positive she say it was a year ago
that they had a meeting in Manning, about a year ago.

Q. Are you interested in them having equal but separate schools?

A. Definitely I am.

Q. Are you interested in sending them to white schools?

A. No Sir, she said she didn't sign for children going to school
together, that what she wanted was equal schools. I don't think
she had that in her mind, course I don't read her mind.

Q. Are you interested in separate schools?

A. I live in all parts of the State and deal with my class. I am
head of the house-hold and have no interest in sending my children
to white schools.

Q. You want them equal but separate?

A. Yes sir, I am going to the next PTA meeting and I want four men
to specially be there and I am going to tell them something. I am
going to let them know exactly how I feel about it.

Q. Have you ever heard of Billy Flemming?

A. I heard about Billy Flemming last night when I talked to my wife.
I talked to her about 1:00 in the morning and she told me he was
selling clothes and food and why she didn't tell me before I don't
know. Different people know it here and there and I told her that
I would have gone there and bought something from him just to be
getting something.
